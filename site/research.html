Overview

Every framework we publish begins as research

The Research Centre is the part of SWAMITRA Foundation that establishes what is actually true before anything is recommended. India has no consolidated evidence base on legal literacy, constitutional awareness or institutional safety practice. Decisions are therefore made on assumption, and programmes are designed around what feels urgent rather than what is measurably absent.

We work in one sequence, without exception. We measure a baseline, publish the finding, build a framework that responds to it, and then return to measure whether the framework changed anything. Where the evidence does not support a recommendation, we say so and publish the gap rather than filling it with opinion.

Everything the Centre produces is released without charge. A school in a district town and a ministry in a capital city should be reading the same document, in the same words, with the same evidence attached to it.

Objectives

What the Research Centre is here to produce

Six standing commitments that define the Centre's work programme and against which its output is reviewed each year.

  1. Generate primary evidence. Conduct national and regional surveys, field studies and institutional assessments that establish where legal and constitutional awareness actually stands.
  2. Publish national frameworks and standards. Convert evidence into implementable standards that any school, workplace or public body can adopt voluntarily.
  3. Produce accessible policy analysis. Write policy briefs and papers that a decision-maker without a legal background can read in one sitting and act on.
  1. Build academic collaboration. Work with universities, law schools and research centres so that findings are contested, replicated and improved.
  2. Maintain an open knowledge repository. Keep every publication, dataset description and framework version permanently available and free.
  3. Develop research capability. Support early-career researchers and institutional research cells working on constitutional and socio-legal questions.

National Frameworks & Standards

Standards any institution may adopt, without charge

A framework is the point at which research becomes something an institution can actually do on a Monday morning.

How a framework is developed

Development begins with a baseline study establishing what institutions currently do, followed by a review of existing statutory and regulatory obligations so that the standard supports the law rather than duplicating it. A drafting group then produces a version zero, which is tested in a small number of volunteering institutions before anything is published.

Consultation is deliberately wide: practitioners who will have to implement the standard, subject experts who can identify what it gets wrong, and the people the standard is meant to protect. Comments received during consultation are recorded, and material changes are reflected in the version history published with the document.

How a framework is revised

Every framework carries a version number and a review date. Minor revisions correcting errors or clarifying language are issued as point releases. A full revision is triggered by a change in law, by implementation evidence showing that a requirement does not work in practice, or at the end of the stated review cycle, whichever comes first. Superseded versions remain downloadable so that an institution can see exactly what changed and when.

Adoption is voluntary. Nothing in a SWAMITRA framework replaces a statutory obligation or constitutes legal advice on a specific matter.

Surveys & Reports

Primary data collection

Surveys are the Centre's main instrument for establishing a baseline. Each survey is published with its questionnaire, sampling approach and known limitations so that findings can be checked rather than simply believed.

National baseline surveys

Large-sample studies measuring awareness of rights, duties and grievance mechanisms across states, education stages and institution types.

Institutional assessments

Structured audits of school and workplace practice against published standards, producing comparable scores rather than descriptive impressions.

Practitioner studies

Interview and focus-group research with teachers, committee members and community facilitators on what actually obstructs correct practice.

Anonymised survey datasets are released after the corresponding report is published. Requests for underlying data may be made through the collaboration form below and are reviewed against our data-sharing and participant-consent conditions.

Research Collaboration

Submit a research proposal

Proposals aligned with the Centre's themes are reviewed on a rolling basis. Use this form to propose a joint study, request access to data, or offer institutional field access.

What we look for

  • A question that is currently unanswered in the Indian context
  • A method proportionate to the claim being made
  • Ethical clearance and safeguarding arrangements where people, and especially children, are involved
  • A commitment to publishing findings openly, including inconvenient ones
  • A realistic timeline with identified institutional support

Proposals are acknowledged on receipt. Review typically takes several weeks, and we write back either way. Collaboration does not carry an automatic funding commitment; funding arrangements, where any exist, are discussed separately and disclosed in the published output.
